Significance statement. Climate change has the potential to reshape the global patterns
of human movement within this century. Nevertheless, existing models focus primarily
on population distributions, neglecting the dynamic interplay of environmental and socio-
economic drivers. We introduce the Feature-Enriched Radiation Model (FERM), which
integrates these external factors to project large-scale migration more accurately. FERM
demonstrates how climate conditions dynamically influence mobility, revealing signifi-
cant shifts in migration patterns by 2070 under severe climate scenarios. Tested in Italy
and the United States, this model provides an adaptable tool for forecasting future mi-
grations, aiding policymakers and planners in developing strategies for urban resilience,
resource management, and climate adaptation. The FERM bridges a critical gap in un-
derstanding human dispersal in an era of rapid environmental change.

Throughout history, billions of people around the world have moved in search of better liv-21
ing conditions, driven by a complex interplay of factors. While economic opportunities and22
political instability have long been recognized as primary motivators, today’s large-scale mi-23
gration is increasingly shaped by profound and accelerating environmental changes. As global24
transformations in climate, resources, and demographics intensify, the need to understand the25
patterns and drivers of human movement has become more pressing. Accurately forecasting26
migration in response to environmental shifts—such as those projected under future climate27
scenarios—is critical for developing resilient urban infrastructures, informed policy decisions,28
and effective climate adaptation strategies.29
Migration today impacts a broad range of sectors, influencing urban planning [1, 2, 3, 4],30

influenced by both socio-economic and environmental pressures, calls for the development of33

These socio-economic, political, and environmental conditions act as exogenous drivers on37
individual decisions [17], shaping the emergence of complex mobility patterns that are often38
difficult to capture with traditional models. One of the earliest attempts to formally define the39
forces driving migration dates back to Ravenstein’s work in 1885 and 1889 [18, 19]. Raven-40
stein’s ”laws of migration,” grounded in social and economic principles, established a founda-41
tion for migration models. These ideas inspired the gravity model, introduced by Schneider in42
1959 [20], which predicts migration flows as proportional to population at origin and destina-43
tion and inversely proportional to distance [21, 22, 23]. This model remains a cornerstone for44
understanding migration and trade patterns.45
More recently, the Radiation Model (RM) has emerged as a powerful alternative to tradi-46
tional migration models, such as the Gravity Model, by providing a physically grounded ap-47
proach to human mobility that avoids the need for empirical parameter calibration [24]. Rooted48
in first principles, the RM aligns with key sociological theories, including the effects of dis-49
tance [25] and intervening opportunities [26]. In this framework, geographic locations are con-50
ceptualized as sources that emit particles — analogous to individuals — each of which has a51
defined probability of being absorbed by nearby locations. This probability is determined ex-52
clusively by the spatial distribution of the population, making population density the model’s53
sole input requirement. The Radiation Model (RM), despite its simplicity and ability to address54
limitations of earlier models, relies solely on population as a driver of mobility, excluding crit-55

ical exogenous factors like environmental conditions or policies. This restricts its applicability56
under changing socio-environmental contexts. Extensions to the RM have sought to overcome57
these limitations: the Radiation Model with selection [27] refined intervening opportunities;58
Kang et al.[28] introduced spatial scaling and directional constraints; Davis et al.[29] incor-59
porated flood-induced migration; and Alis et al. [30] replaced population with an urbanization60

Here, we introduce the Features-Enriched Radiation Model (FERM), an advanced exten-63
sion of the original Radiation Model that explicitly incorporates exogenous drivers, such as64
environmental and socio-economic factors, shaping the emergence of complex mobility pat-65
terns. FERM preserves the foundational principles of the Radiation Model but significantly66
expands its scope, adjusting mobility flows in response to the attractiveness assigned to each67
location based on its unique features. While the core emission/absorption mechanism remains68
unchanged, the underlying stochastic process is modified so that the probability of absorp-69
tion reflects the location-specific attractiveness, dynamically influenced by external conditions.70
FERM thus provides a mathematically robust alternative to the original Radiation Model and its71
currently variations, providing greater adaptability when location-specific factors are essential72
to accurately capture mobility flows. In the following sections, we present the mathematical73
formulation of FERM and the numerical methods used to compute the resulting mobility net-74
work structure. To illustrate the adaptability of the model, we first demonstrate its behavior in75
synthetic spatial environments with self-similar distributions, capturing key structural dynam-76
ics. We then apply FERM to real-world case studies in Italy and the United States, deriving77
interregional mobility flows based on projected climatic conditions for 2070. In our scenario78
analysis, parameters are intentionally set to mirror the spatial distribution of location features,79
without calibration to specific datasets. We show that with optimal parameterization, FERM80

The Feature-enriched Radiation Model. To allow for the inclusion of exogenous factors85
in migration dynamics, the Feature-Enriched Radiation Model (FERM) extends the Radiation86
Model (see Materials and Methods for details) by introducing flexibility in the benefit distribu-87
tion across locations (Supplementary Figure 1). In FERM, each location is assigned a unique88
benefit distribution that reflects its specific features, such as environmental suitability or eco-89
nomic opportunity. These distributions are tailored based on external parameters, allowing the90
model to adjust the probability of absorption dynamically in response to these location-specific91
features. The mathematical structure of FERM maintains the core physical process of the Ra-92
diation Model: particles are emitted from an origin location and are absorbed at a destination.93
However, the distribution of these particles is now influenced not only by population size but94
also by the attractiveness of each location, encoded in parameters that represent exogenous95
features. This additional layer of information enables FERM to capture the effects of diverse96
external drivers—such as climate, infrastructure, and policy—on mobility flows, thus providing97
a nuanced, feature-responsive approach to migration modeling.98

spectively, to reflect the unique characteristics of each location. Mathematically, the parameters103

Discussion254
We introduced the Features-Enriched Radiation Model (FERM), a mathematical generalization255
of the Radiation Model, to incorporate exogenous factors as key drivers of large-scale human256
mobility. FERM broadens the original model by modifying benefit distributions to account for257
location-specific features while preserving its core processes. By demonstrating equivalence258
with the Radiation Model under uniform conditions, FERM ensures theoretical consistency259
while expanding its scope to include complex environmental and socio-economic dynamics.260
Applying FERM to Italy and the United States, we generated scenarios based on shifts in the261
human climate niche [34], showing how external factors can dynamically influence migration262
patterns. Unlike the original Radiation Model, FERM captures how less populated regions can263
become attractive due to external drivers, such as improved climate suitability. These findings264
emphasize FERM’s flexibility in addressing conditions where the Radiation Model struggles,265
such as urban environments and low-income regions [28].266
Future work should refine FERM through parameter calibration using historical data. A267
hierarchical Bayesian framework could enhance its adaptability, treating drivers like climate268
suitability, GDP, and policies as conditional distributions to isolate their effects [38]. This ap-269
proach is particularly valuable given FERM’s capacity to distinguish ”push” and ”pull” factors.270
For instance, climate-induced events like flooding act as ”push” factors, driving out-migration271
from highly exposed regions [39], while improved conditions elsewhere act as ”pull” factors.272
By capturing both dynamics, FERM provides a nuanced framework for modeling migration273
pressures.274

Historical insights from ancient human mobility [40] offer valuable context. Early humans275

These findings have practical implications for policy. Policymakers should prioritize resilience-282

FERM’s ability to integrate diverse drivers, coupled with its flexibility, makes it well-suited287

Materials and Methods291
To make this study self-contained, we revisit the standard Radiation Model (RM) and its limitations,292
which motivated our generalization. The RM provides a parameter-free framework for modeling com-293
muting and migration flows, requiring only population distribution data [24]. By treating population as294
a proxy for migration drivers, it integrates push and pull factors into a single variable, effectively cap-295
turing certain socio-economic influences, such as employment opportunities. However, this simplicity296
comes at the cost of excluding critical exogenous factors, such as cultural, political, geographic, and297
environmental conditions [30].298
This reliance on population alone limits the model’s capacity to capture migration responses to exter-299
nal changes unrelated to population density. Consequently, the RM struggles to model human mobility300
under complex socio-environmental scenarios. To address these challenges, we extend the RM’s math-301
ematical framework to integrate diverse external conditions that shape migration dynamics, broadening302
its applicability to modern migration research.303

To enhance efficiency, we adopt a more streamlined approach by directly sampling a single value373
from the distribution of the maximum (DM), or largest order statistic distribution, of the random vari-374
ablesX andY . When FX(xi|θi) andFY (y|θj) are known, the DM can theoretically be derived, but375
its form is typically complex, requiring an advanced sampling algorithm. For this, we employ the Adap-376
tive Rejection Metropolis Sampling (ARMS) algorithm [41], which enables repeated sampling from the377
maximum distribution efficiently. Using ARMS, we obtain values for absorbance and absorption thresh-378
old, and the right-hand side of eq. (1) is simplified to a comparison between these two quantities. If the379
sampled absorbance exceeds the absorption threshold, the particleQ with absorption thresholdxi will be380
directed towards locationj. Repeating this process across pairs of locations yields the relative frequency381
of movement between each origin-destination pair, approximating the mobility patterns.382
The sampled values are determined by both the family and parametrization of the benefit distribution.383
A right-skewed distribution increases the likelihood of higher values, indicating greater attractiveness for384
that location. Conversely, flatter distributions result in more homogeneous origin-destination (OD) ma-385
trices, while sharply peaked distributions concentrate flows toward the most attractive nearby locations.386
Given these foundational properties, the Gaussian distribution serves as a natural choice for the387
benefit distribution, providing maximum entropy under constraints on mean and variance. However, the388
optimal estimation of model parameters remains a challenging task, necessitating further refinement. In389
the Discussion, we discuss potential strategies to address this issue. For the scenario analyses, we employ390
a set of parameters designed to explore a range of different mobility scenarios.391
